# Today V2 Validation Questions

What must be learned from real students before moving from the current Today screen toward the V2 concept direction. Ordered by what would most change the product if the answer surprises us.

**Status:** Draft. These are research questions, not implementation tasks.

**Context:** See [Today V2 Concept Note (Draft)](/docs/product/today-v2-concept-note-draft) for the future direction these questions validate, and [Today Screen Product Principles (Current)](/docs/product/today-screen-product-principles-current) for the governing principles.

---

## Highest Priority: Would Change the Product Direction

### 1. Do students want priority-first or timeline-first?

**The assumption:** Students care more about "what needs my attention" than "what time is my next class." The V2 concept prioritizes urgency over chronology.

**What would invalidate this:** If students consistently prefer a chronological view and find priority-based ordering confusing or anxiety-inducing. Some students may use the timeline as a grounding ritual — "here's my day, start to finish" — and reordering it by urgency could feel disorienting.

**How to test:**
- Show 5-10 students two mockups: timeline-first (current) vs. attention-first (concept). Ask which one they'd check between classes.
- Watch where students' eyes go on the current screen. Do they look at the DueTodayBanner first, or do they scroll through the timeline?
- Ask: "When you open an app to check your day, what's the first thing you want to know?" Open-ended, don't lead.

**Signal to watch for:** If students say "what time is my next class" more than "am I forgetting anything," the timeline-first model may actually be right for the *current* product, and awareness features should layer on top rather than replace the structure.

### 2. Does "Free until X" create false confidence?

**The assumption:** Showing "Free until 2:30" when assignments are due creates a dangerous sense of calm that could lead to missed deadlines.

**What would invalidate this:** If students treat the hero as schedule-only (when's my next class?) and look elsewhere for task urgency. In that case, the hero doesn't need to carry urgency — it just needs to not actively mislead.

**How to test:**
- Show a student the current screen with "Free until 2:30" and stat pills showing "3 due." Ask what they'd do next.
- Ask: "If you saw this screen, would you feel like you have things to do, or would you feel relaxed?"
- Track behavior: do students who see "Free time" hero states check their tasks less often?

**Signal to watch for:** If students say "I'd still check my tasks, the hero is just telling me about classes" — then the stat pills may be sufficient and the hero doesn't need urgency injection. If students say "I'd probably relax" — then the false confidence problem is real.

### 3. What do students actually do in the first 10 seconds?

**The assumption:** Students want a quick glanceable check. The screen should deliver value in 5-15 seconds.

**What would validate/invalidate:** If students actually spend 30-60 seconds scrolling through the timeline on each visit, the "5-second glance" model is wrong and the screen should be optimized for a slightly longer engagement pattern.

**How to test:**
- Session recording (with consent) on the current Today screen. Measure time-on-screen and scroll depth.
- Ask: "Walk me through what you do when you open DormWay." Watch, don't prompt.
- Compare morning checks vs. between-class checks vs. evening checks — different times may have different patterns.

**Signal to watch for:** Distinct usage modes. If morning = longer planning check and between-class = quick glance, the screen might need to adapt by time of day (or at minimum, the design should serve the quick-glance case without penalizing the planning case).

## High Priority: Would Shape the Implementation

### 4. Do students notice when things change?

**The assumption:** Change detection ("a deadline moved," "a new assignment appeared") is a high-value feature that justifies engineering effort.

**What would invalidate this:** If students don't actually experience frequent mid-day changes, or if they already have reliable channels for change notification (Canvas push notifications, class GroupMe, email), then building change detection is solving a problem students have already solved.

**How to test:**
- Ask: "In the last week, did any deadline or assignment change after you first learned about it? How did you find out?"
- Ask: "Has DormWay ever shown you something that was different from what you expected? What did you do?"
- Survey: "How often do your professors change deadlines or assignments after initially posting them?" (Never / Once a semester / Monthly / Weekly / Constantly)

**Signal to watch for:** Frequency matters. If changes are rare (once a semester), change detection is low-value. If they're weekly or more — especially if students are missing them — it's high-value.

### 5. What does "overwhelmed" look like, and does the app help or hurt?

**The assumption:** During high-stress periods (midterms, finals, heavy weeks), the Today screen should adapt — showing less, focusing on the one next action, breaking paralysis.

**What would invalidate this:** If overwhelmed students want *more* information (the full picture so they can plan), not less. Some students cope by seeing everything and making a plan; for them, a simplified "just do this one thing" screen could feel patronizing.

**How to test:**
- Ask students during a high-stress week: "Show me how you're managing right now. What's helping?"
- Ask: "When you're behind on multiple things, what would you want an app to tell you?" Open-ended.
- Show two mockups: (A) "You have 7 items due this week. Here's the next one." vs. (B) Full list, sorted by urgency. Ask which is more helpful.

**Signal to watch for:** This may split by student type. ADHD/executive-function students may strongly prefer the "one next thing" model. High-achievers may want the full list. If so, this is a preference, not a default.

### 6. What customization do students actually want?

**The assumption:** Students want to control how information is displayed — which courses matter more, how early to see deadlines, what sections to show/hide.

**What would invalidate this:** If students don't customize anything and the defaults are sufficient. Many apps offer customization that <5% of users touch. If DormWay students are in that camp, engineering effort on preferences is wasted.

**How to test:**
- Ask: "If you could change one thing about how DormWay shows your day, what would it be?"
- Ask: "Are there things DormWay shows you that you don't care about? Things you wish were more prominent?"
- Offer 3 simple preference options (deadline lead time, course priority, section visibility) in a prototype. Track adoption rate.

**Signal to watch for:** If students have strong opinions about specific things ("I wish I could see my CS homework earlier"), that validates targeted preferences. If they shrug and say "it's fine," invest in better defaults instead of preference UI.

### 7. How do students use DormWay across devices?

**The assumption:** Students use iPhone for quick checks and iPad/Mac for planning sessions, and the Today screen should adapt density accordingly.

**What would invalidate this:** If students only use DormWay on iPhone and never open it on iPad or Mac, then multi-platform adaptation is premature optimization.

**How to test:**
- Analytics: what percentage of active users have sessions on multiple device types?
- Ask iPad/Mac owners: "Do you use DormWay on your iPad/Mac? Why or why not?"
- If they don't: "What would make you open DormWay on your iPad when you're studying?"

**Signal to watch for:** If <10% of users are multi-device, focus entirely on iPhone. If iPad usage correlates with study sessions, that validates the "desk mode = denser" hypothesis.

## Medium Priority: Would Refine the Details

### 8. Is the "Head to Class" 30-minute threshold right?

**The assumption:** 30 minutes before class, the hero switches to "Head to [location]."

**How to test:** Ask students how far in advance they typically leave for class. Compare against campus walking-time data already in the system. A student whose next class is 3 minutes away doesn't need a 30-minute heads-up.

**Better model:** Use actual walking time when available (the schedule timeline already computes `walkingTimeMinutes` for eligible transitions per SoT - Home & Widgets). Show "Head to class" at `walkingTime + 5 minutes` buffer, not a flat 30 minutes.

### 9. Do students want to complete tasks from Today?

**The assumption:** Inline task completion (checkboxes on the Today screen) would be valuable.

**How to test:** Watch if students who see "3 due today" immediately navigate to a task view to check things off, or if they leave the app and go to Canvas/their own system. If they navigate to tasks within DormWay, inline completion saves a tap. If they leave the app, the problem is bigger than a missing checkbox.

### 10. What does "you're clear" feel like?

**The assumption:** When there's nothing due and no conflicts, the empty state should feel like a reward.

**How to test:** Show students a "You're clear — nothing needs attention" state. Ask: "Do you trust this? Would you stop checking after seeing this?"

**Signal to watch for:** If students say "I'd still check Canvas just in case," DormWay hasn't earned full trust yet, and the "you're clear" state should be qualified ("Based on Canvas and your 3 syllabi, you're clear").

## How to Use This Document

1. **Before building any V2 feature,** check whether the relevant validation question has been answered.
2. **If the answer surprises you,** update the [Today V2 Concept Note (Draft)](/docs/product/today-v2-concept-note-draft) to reflect reality.
3. **Don't treat assumptions as validated** just because they sound reasonable. The whole point of this doc is to flag where we're guessing.
4. **Lightweight validation is fine.** Most of these can be answered with 5-10 student conversations, not formal research. The goal is to reduce the risk of building the wrong thing, not to achieve statistical significance.
